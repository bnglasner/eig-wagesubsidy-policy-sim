# Bonin, Kempe & Schneider (2003) — Kombilohn oder Workfare? Zur Wirksamkeit zweier arbeitsmarktpolitischer Strategien

- **Tier:** 1 (Vierteljahrshefte zur Wirtschaftsforschung / Quarterly Journal of Economic Research; IZA-affiliated authors)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.3790/vjh.72.1.51
- **Source:** IZA DP 587 (working-paper version), https://docs.iza.org/dp587.pdf ; published
  version Vierteljahrshefte zur Wirtschaftsforschung 72(1):51-67
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — the requested German "Kombilohn" wage-subsidy evidence)
- **Evidence class:** Modeled/ex-ante — microsimulation comparing two labor-market-policy strategies.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Simulates and compares two strategies for activating German welfare recipients:
**Kombilohn** (wage/combination-wage subsidies that top up low earnings, structurally a
wage-subsidy family member) versus **workfare** (mandatory work requirements for benefit
receipt). Central result: wage subsidies (Kombilohn) produce only a **marginal increase in
labor-force participation**, and what participation gain occurs is significantly offset by
**negative work-time (intensive-margin) effects** — some earners reduce hours in response
to the subsidy's implicit marginal tax rate on additional earnings — plus **high fiscal
cost and deadweight loss** (subsidizing earnings that would have been earned anyway).
Workfare, by contrast, is found to produce a larger net positive employment effect at
comparable or lower fiscal cost in this simulation framework, because it does not carry the
same work-time disincentive.

## Why this matters for the decomposition question
This is a **genuine wage-subsidy-specific, non-EITC data point**, and it points the
opposite direction from the "wage subsidies should structurally outperform the EITC's
participation response" thesis: even a subsidy explicitly free of the EITC's annual-lump-
sum, phase-out-cliff design still produces only a **marginal** participation effect once
its own implicit marginal-tax-rate structure and behavioral offsets are modeled. This is
important evidence that **some of the muted response the project's articles attribute to
the EITC's specific frictions may instead be a more general property of earnings-linked
transfers** (any positive-sloped or plateau-then-phase-out schedule creates an implicit
tax on effort that dampens some of the gross participation gain) — i.e., evidence for the
"soft prior, not a clean decomposition" reading of the research question.

## Worker vs. employer payment (2026-07-08 addendum, requested re-verification; full IZA
DP 587 text now read directly, pp. 6-11)
**Confirmed: WORKER-side.** The paper explicitly considers, then *rejects for
implementation-quality reasons*, delivering the subsidy as a reduction in the worker's own
social-insurance contributions ("Nachlass auf die Arbeitnehmerbeiträge zur
Sozialversicherung") — because that route contaminates pension entitlements and drags in
existing means-tested transfer clawbacks. The specification the paper actually simulates
(p. 8, describing the Nordrhein-Westfalen labor-ministry model on which it is based)
instead has the welfare/tax authority ("Sozialämter oder Steuerbehörden") pay a subsidy
**directly to the worker**, scaled to the worker's own gross earnings — i.e., a cash
top-up on the worker's paycheck, delivered on the same logic as a Mainzer-Modell/WFTC-style
earnings supplement, not a hiring-cost rebate to the firm. This is the opposite delivery
channel from Huttunen-Pirttilä-Uusitalo (2013)'s Finnish employer-side payroll subsidy
(also in this catalog): Bonin et al.'s Kombilohn is worker-visible and paycheck-linked, the
same delivery axis the 80-80 relies on. **This makes the paper's near-zero/negative result
a materially stronger caution for the 80-80's design than a same-conclusion employer-side
paper would be** — the null cannot be dismissed here as a demand-side-incidence artifact.

## Household-composition conditioning (2026-07-08 addendum, requested re-verification)
**Confirmed: NONE — universal by individual/household earnings, not by presence of
children.** Eligibility and the subsidy schedule scale with the worker's (or household's)
gross earnings only (Abbildung 2/3, pp. 7-8); the worked example is explicitly a
*"kinderloser Alleinstehender"* (childless single person, p. 4), used precisely because it
is the simplest case, and the paper separately notes that households *with* children face
a *higher* Sozialhilfe (welfare) floor under the pre-existing transfer system — a fact
about the counterfactual benefit level these workers compare the subsidy against, not a
feature of the subsidy's own eligibility rule. So, like Huttunen et al., this paper's null
result **cannot** be attributed to a categorical/children-based exclusion the 80-80 would
remove — the Kombilohn already lacked that conditioning.

## Striking new finding on gender composition (2026-07-08 addendum, full text read, p. 9)
The simulated net additional labor supply from the subsidy is small in absolute terms
(37,300 persons in the lower funding variant; 104,000 in the upper variant — a **2.3
percent** reduction in the nonemployed stock in the sample even under the most generous
variant) and its *composition* is the opposite of what a naive reading might expect:
**men's aggregate labor supply nets negative** under the subsidy. The mechanism is
intra-household coordination, not an EITC-style categorical exclusion: in couples, the
subsidy is more often claimed by the lower-wage partner (predominantly women), and some
higher-earning male partners then work *less* because the household's income floor is
already secured by the subsidized partner's earnings — a few withdraw from full-time work
entirely once the couple's net income needs are met. Gains are concentrated among women
(net), consistent with the same gendered pattern the EITC produces (participation gains
concentrated among women, near-zero-to-negative for men) — but produced by a completely
different mechanism (household labor-supply coordination under a universal, non-categorical
earnings subsidy) rather than by categorical/children-based credit conditioning. **This is
independent evidence that a non-categorical, worker-side wage subsidy can still reproduce
an EITC-like gendered participation pattern**, which further undercuts (rather than
supports) an expectation that removing EITC's categorical conditioning alone would flip the
"near-zero-for-men" result.

## Caveats
- **Ex-ante microsimulation**, not a measured/ex-post evaluation of an implemented
  program — the magnitudes are model-dependent (labor-supply elasticities assumed in the
  simulation), not directly observed.
- German labor-market and welfare-system institutional context (structural unemployment,
  means-tested Sozialhilfe/ALG II-era benefit interactions) differs substantially from the
  U.S. low-wage labor market the 80-80 targets; the qualitative "implicit marginal tax
  rate offsets participation gains" mechanism is the transportable lesson, not the point
  magnitudes.
- Worker-side delivery and non-categorical eligibility are now both confirmed directly
  from the primary text (IZA DP 587, pp. 4-9) rather than inferred from secondary
  summaries — see addenda above. Point estimates (37,300 / 104,000 net additional labor
  supply; 2.3 percent stock reduction; ~40,000-45,000 euro cost per additional worker; the
  net-negative male labor-supply result) are now pinned directly to the source and no
  longer flagged as unverified.

## Primary-source verification (2026-07-08, second pass — independent re-check)

IZA DP 587 (docs.iza.org/dp587.pdf, 49 pp., German) was re-downloaded and re-read this
pass **without relying on the earlier addenda above**, specifically to re-verify the four
claims the task flagged. Result: **all four confirmed**, with page/table pins and two
small precision notes.

1. **Worker-side delivery — CONFIRMED.** The paper discusses subsidizing worker-side
   social-insurance contributions ("Subvention von Sozialversicherungsbeiträgen auf der
   Arbeitnehmerseite," p. 7, describing the Nordrhein-Westfalen labor-ministry concept),
   then argues that actually reducing the worker's contributions creates pension-claim,
   transparency, and welfare/housing-benefit clawback problems, and concludes it is
   better to leave contributions unchanged and instead have the welfare or tax
   authorities pay out a subsidy based on gross earnings: "die Beitragszahlungen der
   Geringverdiener de facto unverändert zu lassen und stattdessen durch Sozialämter oder
   Steuerbehörden eine vom Bruttoeinkommen ausgehende Subvention auszuzahlen" (p. 8).
   The simulated instrument is thus a cash payment to the worker scaled to the worker's
   own gross earnings — worker-visible, not an employer rebate.
2. **Non-categorical eligibility and the childless-single worked example — CONFIRMED.**
   The subsidy schedule depends on monthly gross earnings only: lower variant = full
   contribution subsidy from the 325-euro mini-job threshold, phasing out at 920
   euros/month; upper variant = full subsidy to 510 euros, phase-out ending at 1,280
   euros/month (Abbildung 2, p. 7). No children or marital test anywhere in the rule.
   The budget-line worked example (Abbildung 1 and pp. 3-5; figure notes pp. 21-22) is
   explicitly a **"kinderloser Alleinstehender in Westdeutschland ohne Kapitaleinkommen"**
   (childless single person in West Germany without capital income), at 7 vs 13
   euros/hour. The prior addendum cited "p. 4" for this example; the discussion spans
   pp. 3-5 with the figure legend confirming the childless-single case.
3. **Men's aggregate labor supply nets NEGATIVE — CONFIRMED, Tabelle 1 (p. 19; source
   line: Schneider et al. 2002b).** Labor-supply changes vs. status quo: **men −4,800
   (lower variant) / −7,500 (upper variant); women +42,100 / +111,500; net +37,300 /
   +104,000.** Mechanism as described (p. 9): household coordination — couples arrange
   labor supply so at least one partner (predominantly the lower-wage woman) claims the
   subsidy, and "in einigen – wenigen – Fällen zieht sich der besser verdienende Ehemann
   sogar vom Arbeitsmarkt zurück" (in a few cases the higher-earning husband withdraws
   from the labor market entirely). The paper additionally finds the contraction strong
   enough that **total work volume (hours) falls** even as net headcount participation
   rises (p. 9) — a stronger statement than the prior summary made explicit.
4. **Net additional labor supply 37,300–104,000 — CONFIRMED** (Tabelle 1 and p. 9 text:
   "In der unteren Variante beträgt das zusätzliche Arbeitsangebot netto 37.300, in der
   oberen Variante 104.000 Personen"), equal to only a **2.3 percent** reduction of the
   nonemployed stock even in the generous variant (p. 9).

Also re-confirmed from the same pass: fiscal cost **40,000–45,000 euros per person
crossing the labor-supply threshold** (p. 10); direct subsidy cost 1.1 / 3.3 billion
euros per year (lower/upper variants) plus 470 / 870 million euros in lost income-tax and
contribution revenue; **~90 percent of outlays are deadweight** (reaching workers already
in the subsidized earnings range or reducing hours, p. 10); restricting the subsidy to
full-time jobs cuts cost per additional worker below 20,000 euros but shrinks the effect
to +31,000 persons (p. 10). One provenance note the prior addenda omitted: **Tabelle 1's
source line attributes the simulation results to Schneider et al. (2002b)** — the
underlying IZA/NRW study — so this paper is the citable summary presentation of those
simulations, not an independent second run.
