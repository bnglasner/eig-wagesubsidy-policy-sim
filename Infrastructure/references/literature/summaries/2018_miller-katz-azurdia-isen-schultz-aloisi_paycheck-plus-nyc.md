## Finding (paraphrased)
Paycheck Plus is a real-world test of a **non-categorical, worker-paid, earnings-linked
credit for workers without dependent children** — over 6,000 low-income single New Yorkers
were randomly assigned to a treatment group eligible for a credit of up to $2,000/year
(roughly four times the federal childless-EITC maximum, with the income cap raised to
$30,000, twice the federal childless limit) or a control group receiving the standard
federal/state childless EITC (about $500 maximum). Over a three-year follow-up: the program
increased after-bonus income and modestly raised **overall employment rates by 2.1-2.6
percentage points** in years two and three (no significant effect in year one). This
overall effect was **concentrated in two subgroups**: women (up to 4.6 percentage points)
and "**disadvantaged men**" — a subgroup defined to include **noncustodial parents and
formerly incarcerated individuals** — who saw employment gains of up to **5.8 percentage
points**. Effects on hours/earnings among those already working were small; the program
also increased tax filing and child-support payment among noncustodial parents, and reduced
severe poverty by 3.4 percentage points, though a contemporaneous critique (Laura and John
Arnold Foundation's Evidence-Based Policy team, "Straight Talk on Evidence," 2018) flags
that with ~10 measured primary outcomes, some secondary findings (notably the severe-
poverty result) do not survive a Benjamini-Hochberg multiple-comparisons correction; the
critique does **not** dispute the employment finding itself but argues MDRC's public
framing understated null results on total household income and overall poverty.

## Why this matters for the household-composition-channel question (the most directly on-point evidence found)
This is the closest thing in the literature to a clean experimental test of the user's
hypothesis, because Paycheck Plus removes the EITC's small-childless-credit conditioning
(quadrupling the maximum and doubling the income cap for the *same* childless-worker
population) while keeping the delivery worker-side and earnings-linked, much like the
80-80. The result is a genuinely **mixed** signal, not a clean confirmation:
- **In favor of the hypothesis:** overall employment did rise, and rose most for
  "disadvantaged men" — a population that substantially overlaps with the household-
  composition-excluded population the user is asking about (noncustodial fathers are, by
  definition, parents who are ineligible for the *standard* EITC specifically because of a
  custody/residency test, not an earnings test). A 5.8-point gain for this subgroup is far
  above the EITC-implied ~0.05-type near-zero response for men in this project's existing
  calibration. This is real, causal (RCT), and population-specific evidence that removing
  a specific household-composition exclusion (custody status) can move employment for the
  men it was excluding.
- **Against an unqualified reading:** the effect is *not* a general "childless men respond
  more once you remove EITC's categorical conditioning" result — it is concentrated in a
  narrower, more disadvantaged sub-slice of men (noncustodial parents, formerly
  incarcerated). The broader "men" cell in this project's model is not equivalent to
  "disadvantaged men" in the MDRC sense, and the Atlanta replication of the same design
  (Yang, Bernardi, Miller, Katz & Isen 2022, separately cataloged) found **no** employment
  effect at all, for any subgroup, in any year — a direct within-literature contradiction
  that must be disclosed alongside this result, not smoothed over.

## Caveats
- **Delivery is a lump-sum tax-time payment, not a per-paycheck subsidy** — Paycheck Plus
  does not test the "real pay"/salience mechanism the project's articles separately
  emphasize; it isolates the *generosity/categorical* channel, not the *timing/salience*
  channel, which is the correct isolation for this specific request but should not be
  over-read as testing the 80-80's full design.
- Voluntary take-up/marketing-driven program, not an automatic tax-code feature — the
  report itself notes uptake required active outreach, unlike a wage subsidy embedded in
  payroll.
- One of two site replications (NYC); the other (Atlanta) found null results — see the
  companion 2022 summary. Any conclusion drawn from this report alone, without the Atlanta
  contrast, would overstate confidence.

## 2026-07-08 addendum — primary report read; employment numbers pinned (other_women band pass)
The primary MDRC report PDF (September 2018) was retrieved and read directly this pass
(title page, Executive Summary ES-1 to ES-10, Table ES.2). This clears the earlier
`[unverified]` flags on the subgroup magnitudes and bibliographic details:
- **Author list and date confirmed from the title page:** Cynthia Miller (MDRC), Lawrence
  F. Katz (Harvard), Gilda Azurdia (MDRC), Adam Isen (U.S. Treasury), Caroline Schultz
  (MDRC), Kali Aloisi (MDRC); September 2018.
- **Table ES.2 (IRS records, n = 5,968), employment-rate effects in percentage points:**
  - Full sample: Year 1 +0.9 (ns); Year 2 **+2.6***\*\*; Year 3 **+2.1**\*\*; Years 1-3
    **+1.9**\*\* (77.3 vs 75.4).
  - **All women:** Year 1 +2.3\*; Year 2 **+4.6**\*\*\*; Year 3 +2.6\*; **Years 1-3 +3.2\*\*\***
    (83.2 vs 80.0). The previously secondary-sourced "up to 4.6pp" is confirmed as the
    Year-2 estimate, significant at the 1 percent level.
  - **All men:** Years 1-3 +0.5 (ns; -0.3/+0.7/+1.2 by year); women-vs-men subgroup
    difference significant at the 10 percent level (†).
  - **More disadvantaged men** (defined in the report as men ever incarcerated or
    noncustodial parents ordered to pay child support): Year 3 **+5.8**\*\* (62.4 vs 56.6,
    a 10 percent increase); Years 1-3 +2.8 (ns). **Other men:** Year 3 -1.3 (ns), Years
    1-3 -0.6 (ns); Year-3 difference across the two male subgroups significant (††).
- **Sample composition confirmed (ES-4):** 59 percent men, 22 percent without a high
  school diploma, 18 percent ever incarcerated, 9 percent noncustodial parents; all
  participants single adults without dependent children earning under $30,000.
- Bonus receipt fell from 64 percent (Year 1) to 54 percent (Year 3) of eligibles; average
  bonus about $1,400 — take-up friction relevant when mapping to an automatic per-paycheck
  design.
**Relevance to the `other_women` cell:** the "all women" rows are the only verified
experimental estimate in this catalog of CHILDLESS SINGLE women's employment response to a
non-categorical, worker-side, individually-assessed earnings credit: **+3.2pp pooled
(p<0.01), the largest and most robust subgroup effect in the study** — women, not
disadvantaged men, carry the NYC result on the pooled horizon. Must still be read against
the Atlanta null (Yang et al. 2022, cataloged), where women's effects were also null.

## Primary-source verification (2026-07-08, second pass)

Independent second pass, run separately from the addendum above (which covers Table ES.2):
the full report PDF (mdrc.org/sites/default/files/PaycheckPlus_FinalReport.pdf; mirrored at
nyc.gov/assets/opportunity/pdf/evidence/paycheckpluseval-2018.pdf) was downloaded and
converted to text; the Executive Summary **and the body chapters (Tables 10-12, footnotes
49-50)** were read directly. Everything below is VERIFIED-PRIMARY unless flagged.

### The "up to 5.8 percentage points" claim — CONFIRMED, correctly characterized
- **Table ES.2 and body Table 10 (p. 36), "Any earnings," more disadvantaged men:**
  Year 3 = 62.4 program vs 56.6 control → **+5.8pp, significant at 5 percent (\*\*)**;
  the report's own text (ES-7 and p. 34) states "increased employment rates in Year 3 by
  5.8 percentage points (or 10 percent)." Year 1 +0.6 (ns), Year 2 +2.1 (ns), Years 1-3
  pooled +2.8 (ns) — so **"up to 5.8pp" is accurate and the qualifier "Year 3 only" is
  mandatory**; the pooled effect for this subgroup is not significant.
- **Subgroup Ns (Table 10):** records sample 618 program / 645 control disadvantaged men
  (survey sample 295/301); all-men records total = 3,409.
- **Precise definition (p. 33 and Table 10 note a):** men who at random assignment were
  noncustodial parents with an open child support (IV-D) case owing support or arrears,
  **or** had been incarcerated at some point prior to random assignment. Overlap
  (footnote 50): ~11 percent both, 63 percent formerly incarcerated only, 26 percent
  noncustodial only. The earlier (2017 interim) report analyzed the two groups separately;
  they were pooled in the final report for precision, with separate results in Appendix
  Tables A.4/A.5 (Year-3 employment effects "very similar" for both; earnings effects
  much larger for noncustodial parents).
- **Standard errors: NOT retrievable from this report.** The NYC final report prints
  significance stars only (\*\*\* 1%, \*\* 5%, \* 10%; †/††/††† for subgroup-impact
  differences), no SEs or p-values, for the employment tables. `[unverified: SE/p-value
  of the 5.8pp estimate — the primary report does not publish them; the Atlanta companion
  report does publish SEs, but NYC does not.]`
- Companion Year-3 earnings effect for disadvantaged men: **+$1,205 (~13 percent), "just
  misses statistical significance at the 10 percent level"** (p. 34, Table 10).

### Other numbers verified this pass (beyond the ES.2 addendum above)
- **Year 1 = calendar 2014, before any bonus was paid** (p. 34) — Year-1 nulls are
  expected mechanically; Years 2-3 are the informative estimates.
- **Referral decomposition (footnote 49):** without the employment-referral component,
  overall employment effects are estimated at 1.9pp (Year 2) and 1.3pp (Year 3) vs the
  observed 2.6/2.1; with the referral extended to the whole low-earnings group, 3.5/2.5.
  Relevant when mapping to a pure bonus with no service component.
- **Severe poverty:** −3.4pp\*\* (29.2 vs 32.6 percent below 50 percent of the poverty
  line; Table ES.1 survey outcomes) — the number quoted in the Finding section is
  confirmed, as is the report's framing that the overall poverty rate was unaffected.
- **Program parameters confirmed:** bonus up to $2,000/year, eligibility to $30,000
  earnings, control receives standard federal/state childless EITC (~$500 max);
  recruitment of 6,000+ single adults without dependent children, Sept 2013–Feb 2014;
  analysis N = 5,968.
- **No peer-reviewed journal version of the final impact findings exists** as of this
  pass: a targeted search (NBER, Katz's Harvard publication list, journals) found only
  the MDRC reports (2017 interim, 2018 final) plus peer-reviewed *health-outcome*
  companions (e.g., Paycheck Plus Health Study, Milbank Quarterly 2024) — the MDRC report
  remains the primary and citable source for the employment estimates.

### Corrections to prior characterizations
- None material. The prior summary's numbers (2.1-2.6pp overall; 4.6pp women; 5.8pp
  disadvantaged men; 3.4pp severe poverty) are all confirmed against the primary tables.
  One sharpening: the prior text said the overall effect "was concentrated in two
  subgroups"; per Table ES.2 the pooled-years result is carried by **women (+3.2pp\*\*\*)**,
  while the disadvantaged-men effect is a **Year-3-only** result (pooled ns) — phrase
  load-bearing uses accordingly.
