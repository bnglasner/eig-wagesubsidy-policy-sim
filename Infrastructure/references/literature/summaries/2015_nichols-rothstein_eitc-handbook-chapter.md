## Finding (paraphrased)
A comprehensive review of EITC research since Hotz & Scholz (2003), covering history and
rules, labor-supply incidence, well-being effects, and reform proposals. Confirms the
consensus pattern: labor-supply effects are positive and large for single mothers
(extensive margin), smaller and negative for married mothers, and "essentially
nonexistent" for men. Directly documents the **credit-schedule mechanics of the
household-composition channel**: the 1993 budget introduced a childless-worker schedule
with a maximum credit of only $481 (2013 dollars) — about 15 percent of the one-child
maximum — phasing out at just over $14,000 in income, a schedule that "remains much less
generous than that for families with qualifying children" and that (as of the chapter's
writing) had not been substantially changed since 1994. Also documents that non-custodial
parents are "generally ineligible for the EITC, even if they provide substantial support to
the child" — the specific categorical/custody-based exclusion the 80-80's job-and-hour
eligibility does not carry — and that New York State and Washington, DC have experimented
with EITCs for non-custodial parents specifically to address this gap (see
Nichols-Sorensen-Lippold 2012, separately cataloged).

## Why this matters for the household-composition-channel question (directly on point)
This is the most authoritative single source confirming the *mechanical* magnitude of the
channel the user flagged: the childless credit is not merely "smaller," it is roughly
one-eighth to one-sixth of the parental credit, and was near-zero before 1994. Two further
points bear directly on the decomposition question:
1. **Incidence bears on childless/ineligible workers without offsetting benefit.** The
   chapter's incidence discussion (citing Rothstein 2010, Leigh 2010) finds that a
   meaningful share of the EITC's benefit is captured by employers through reduced pre-tax
   wages for *all* workers in the affected labor market — including workers "not eligible
   for the credit (due, e.g., to not having children)." This means childless/single men who
   compete in the same low-wage labor markets as EITC-eligible parents may bear part of the
   wage-suppression cost of the EITC's parent-favoring generosity without receiving an
   offsetting credit — a structural disadvantage layered *on top of* the smaller credit
   schedule itself, and one a non-categorical subsidy like the 80-80 would not reproduce
   (because it does not preferentially inflate one group's labor supply over another's).
2. **Take-up is also lower for childless filers, independent of credit size.** The chapter
   reports (citing Jones 2014a) that EITC take-up conditional on eligibility was
   substantially lower for filers without children (56-65 percent, rising over
   2005-2009) than for those with children (82-86 percent). This is a *separate*,
   behavioral/salience-adjacent factor (not itself credit-schedule generosity) that further
   dampens the observed childless-worker response and should not be conflated with the
   generosity channel when attributing "why is the childless/male response smaller."
3. **No formal decomposition provided.** The chapter does **not** provide a quantitative
   decomposition splitting the parent-vs-childless labor-supply-response gap into a
   "credit-generosity" component versus a "different underlying elasticity by family type"
   component. It documents the facts that would feed such a decomposition (schedule ratio,
   incidence spillover, take-up gap) but does not itself estimate one. This is the same
   "no clean decomposition available" conclusion this project's earlier literature-scout
   pass reached from other sources — reinforced, not overturned, by this more
   comprehensive review.

## Caveats
- A synthesis/handbook chapter, not a new causal estimate — its evidentiary weight rests on
  the underlying studies it cites (Meyer & Rosenbaum 2001, Eissa & Hoynes 2004, Rothstein
  2010, Leigh 2010, Jones 2014a, all separately verifiable), most of which pre-date the
  2021 ARPA childless-EITC expansion and the Paycheck Plus demonstrations (see separately
  cataloged entries for both).
- Written in 2015; does not cover the 2021 American Rescue Plan Act's temporary childless-
  EITC expansion or the final Paycheck Plus reports (2018 NYC, 2022 Atlanta), all of which
  postdate it and are cataloged separately.
- DOI for the published chapter could not be validated this pass (see above); citations
  here rely on the NBER working-paper version, which was read directly and is textually
  near-identical to the published chapter's early sections per cross-referencing with the
  chapter's listed page range (137-218) and table of contents structure.
