## Finding (paraphrased, read from full text)
Phelps argues for a **low-wage employment subsidy paid to firms** (not to workers) based
on the stock of low-wage employees on their payroll, financed by general revenue. His
efficiency-wage-theoretic argument: the welfare state's payroll taxes and safety net raise
the "incentive wage" employers must pay to deter quitting/shirking, which raises the
natural rate of unemployment and falls hardest on the most disadvantaged. A wage subsidy
funded by taxing more advantaged workers would lower this burden and raise both
employment and take-home pay for the low-wage population. **Critically, Phelps explicitly
critiques the EITC** (the "American scheme in place") on two grounds that map directly
onto the project's own articles: (1) by raising labor supply, the EITC **depresses the
market wage employers pay**, imposing a cost on low-wage workers not covered by the
credit; and (2) it privileges "workers with children" over "workers without them . . .
many of them just as disadvantaged." He also explicitly criticizes categorical/targeted
subsidy designs (e.g., unemployment- or welfare-recipient-only wage vouchers) as unfair to
similarly disadvantaged workers excluded by the category, and proposes instead **a
graduated subsidy based on the wage paid to all employees, new and old, in all
categories** — i.e., broad and non-categorical.

## Why this is NOT a clean transportable elasticity source (important caveat)
Two structural mismatches with the 80-80 must be disclosed:
1. **Phelps's proposed mechanism is employer-side** (a subsidy paid to the firm for its
   low-wage payroll), which operates on labor **demand**. The 80-80 is a **worker-side**,
   direct-to-worker wage fill — closer in spirit to Phelps's stated rationale (broad,
   non-categorical) but not to his proposed delivery mechanism.
2. **This is a theoretical/normative argument, not an empirical study.** It contains no
   estimated extensive-margin elasticity, no data, and no identification strategy. It
   cannot be cited as evidence for *how much* a broad, non-categorical, worker-side
   subsidy would move entry — only for the *argument* that the EITC's demographic pattern
   (near-zero for childless/male workers) reflects the EITC's own categorical design
   choice, a claim Phelps makes qualitatively and which the project's articles adopt.

## Why it matters for 80-80
This is the correct primary citation for the project's own stated intellectual lineage
(the case that a broad, non-categorical wage subsidy should outperform the EITC on the
participation margin specifically because it avoids categorical eligibility and does not
suppress the market wage the way a supply-side credit does). It belongs in the brief as
the **theoretical motivation**, explicitly labeled as modeled/normative rather than an
empirical elasticity source — the empirical gap it does not fill is exactly what
Huttunen-Pirttilä-Uusitalo (2013), Bonin-Kempe-Schneider (2003), and Bishop-Montgomery
(1993) (all in this catalog) partially address for the *employer-side* wage-subsidy
family Phelps himself proposed.
