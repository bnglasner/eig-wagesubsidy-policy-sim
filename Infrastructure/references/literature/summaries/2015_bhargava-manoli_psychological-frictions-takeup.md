# Bhargava & Manoli (2015) — Psychological Frictions and the Incomplete Take-Up of Social Benefits: Evidence from an IRS Field Experiment

- **Tier:** 1 (American Economic Review)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1257/aer.20121493
- **Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.20121493
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — payment-salience/take-up literature)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — large randomized field experiment (35,050 EITC
  non-claimants who received an initial IRS notice).

## Finding (paraphrased)
IRS records identified 35,050 tax filers who appeared eligible for but had not claimed
roughly $26 million in EITC benefits despite an initial notice. A randomized follow-up
mailing experiment tested which frictions explained the non-claiming: **information/
complexity frictions** (simplified explanations, increased benefit salience) versus
**hassle-cost/stigma frictions** (reducing perceived audit risk, application burden,
stigma). Result: **simplification and increased salience produced substantial additional
claiming; interventions aimed at reducing stigma, audit fear, or application effort did
not.** The paper concludes that low program awareness/understanding and informational
complexity — not stigma or hassle costs — are the dominant frictions behind incomplete
EITC take-up in this population.

## Why this matters for the decomposition question
This is the strongest available evidence that **payment/schedule salience and formula
complexity are real, quantitatively important frictions** — directly supporting the half
of the project's articles' argument concerned with formula complexity and legibility.
**Important margin distinction:** this experiment is about **take-up of a credit workers
had already earned** (claiming behavior on a completed tax year), not about the
**extensive-margin labor-supply decision** to enter employment in the first place. It is
therefore strong evidence that simplification/salience would likely raise EITC (or 80-80)
**take-up conditional on eligibility**, but it is not direct evidence that removing the
EITC's complexity would by itself close the extensive-margin gap between men/childless
adults and single mothers — that requires the labor-supply response literature (Meyer
2002; McClelland & Mok 2012, both cataloged), where the salience-alone evidence (Chetty &
Saez 2013, cataloged) is a null. Read together, Bhargava-Manoli and Chetty-Saez bracket the
honest answer: **salience clearly moves claiming/take-up; it does not cleanly show up as a
moved labor-supply margin** in the one large randomized test that looked for it.

## Caveats
- Population: tax filers who already had qualifying earnings and simply failed to claim —
  not non-employed individuals. Bears on the "would a legible 80-80 raise take-up among
  the already-eligible" question more than the "would it induce entry" question.
