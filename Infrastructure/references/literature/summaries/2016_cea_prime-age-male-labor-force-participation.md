# Council of Economic Advisers (2016) — The Long-Term Decline in Prime-Age Male Labor Force Participation

- **Tier:** 2 (official government economic research; Executive Office of the President, CEA)
- **Type:** technical_note
- **Source:** https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/page/files/20160620_cea_primeage_male_lfp.pdf (June 2016)
- **Thread:** A / target-population sizing — characterizes the non-employed prime-age MALE pool, the largest 80-80 entry pool, and diagnoses its drivers.
- **Evidence class:** Government synthesis of measured evidence plus original CEA descriptive analysis (no DOI; report).

## Finding (paraphrased)
Documents the long-run fall in prime-age (25–54) male labor force participation and its
composition:
- The decline is **concentrated among less-educated men**: participation fell most for men
  with a **high school degree or less (~14 percentage points)** — the same group that saw
  the largest **relative wage declines**.
- The joint pattern of **falling participation AND falling relative wages** points to a
  **labor-DEMAND-side cause**: a negative demand shock for less-skilled men's labor lowers
  the wages firms offer, and some men decline to work at those wages and exit the labor
  force.
- State-level evidence: prime-age male participation is **higher where the absolute and
  relative returns to work are higher** (higher low-end wages, less low-end wage inequality).
- Alternative (supply-side) explanations are smaller: rising SSDI receipt (prime-age male
  receipt rose from ~1% in 1967 to ~3% in 2014) contributed **at most ~0.3–0.5 pp of the
  ~7.5 pp** participation decline.

## Why it matters for 80-80 (target-population sizing & rationale)
This is the demand-side diagnosis of the 80-80's core target group (childless prime-age
men). Two model implications: (1) it sizes and characterizes the largest entry pool; and
(2) it provides the **policy rationale and a caution**: because the wage signal is depressed
by weak demand, **raising the return to work** (exactly what the 80-80 wage fill does) is
the lever CEA's own analysis implies could pull some non-participants back in — but the
realized supply response should be modest, consistent with the low prime-age participation
elasticities in the CBO/McClelland & Mok entry. Pairs directly with Abraham & Kearney
(2020) on the demand-driven nature of prime-age non-employment.
