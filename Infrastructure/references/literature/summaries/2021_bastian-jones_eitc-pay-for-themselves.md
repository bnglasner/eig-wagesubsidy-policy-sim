# Bastian & Jones (2021) — Do EITC Expansions Pay for Themselves?

- **Tier:** 1 (Journal of Public Economics)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2020.104355
- **Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S004727272030219X ; researchwithrutgers
- **Thread:** B angle 1 (elasticity->revenue feedback / self-financing) — the cleanest measured analogue.
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post quasi-experimental estimates fed into a fiscal-externality accounting.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Estimates the degree to which post-1990s EITC expansions are self-financing once a
wide set of fiscal externalities (higher income/payroll taxes paid, reduced spending on
other transfers due to induced work) is accounted for. Finds the EITC's **net cost is
only ~17 percent of its ~$70 billion budgetary cost** over a one-year horizon — i.e.,
roughly **$0.83 of each EITC dollar is recovered** through behavioral tax-revenue and
transfer-offset channels.

## Conflict/sensitivity note
The high self-financing share is contingent on the large employment elasticities that
Kleven (2024) disputes. If the entry response is near zero, the self-financing share
collapses. Treat ~83% recovery as an upper bound, not a point estimate.

## Why it matters for 80-80 (critical)
This is the direct empirical template for the 80-80's "net of tax recapture and
safety-net responses" question: it operationalizes how to convert induced labor-supply
responses into recovered fiscal cost. The methodology is portable to the 80-80 fill.
