# Abraham & Kearney (2020) — Explaining the Decline in the US Employment-to-Population Ratio: A Review of the Evidence

- **Tier:** 1 (Journal of Economic Literature)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1257/jel.20191480
- **Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.20191480 ; NBER w24333
- **Thread:** A / target-population sizing — characterizes the size and drivers of the non-employed prime-age pool that the 80-80 would draw from.
- **Evidence class:** Review/synthesis of measured (ex-post) evidence; the authors also assemble a quantitative accounting of contributing factors.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Reviews the decline in the US employment-to-population ratio (EPOP):
- Overall EPOP **fell from 64.3% (1999) to 60.4% (2018) — a 3.8 percentage-point decline**,
  driven centrally by **within-age-group employment declines among young and prime-age
  adults** (not just population aging).
- **Labor-DEMAND factors are the most important drivers** of the within-group declines:
  **import competition from China** (the China trade shock reduced EPOP by ~0.92 pp) and
  **penetration of robots/automation** (the second-largest factor).
- **Supply-side / institutional factors play smaller roles**: increased disability-insurance
  participation (less important but not inconsequential), and minimum-wage increases and
  rising incarceration contributed modestly.
- Several factors flagged as unresolved/needing more research: child-care barriers, leisure
  technology, opioids, occupational licensing, declining labor-market fluidity.

## Why it matters for 80-80 (target-population sizing & responsiveness)
The 80-80's largest entry pool is non-employed prime-age workers, especially men, where
the EITC participation evidence does NOT transfer. Abraham & Kearney establish that this
pool's non-employment is **predominantly demand-driven** (trade + automation), which has
two implications for the model: (1) it sizes the pool and explains its growth; and (2) it
cautions that a worker-side wage subsidy faces a population whose non-employment reflects
weak demand for their labor — so the supply (entry) response may be modest, consistent with
the low prime-age participation elasticities (see McClelland & Mok / CBO entry) and with
the CEA (2016) demand-side diagnosis.
