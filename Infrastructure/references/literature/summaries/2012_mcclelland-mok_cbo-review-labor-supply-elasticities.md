# McClelland & Mok (2012, CBO) — A Review of Recent Research on Labor Supply Elasticities

- **Tier:** 2 (official government economic research, CBO Working Paper 2012-12)
- **Type:** technical_note
- **Source:** https://www.cbo.gov/publication/43675 (PDF: cbo.gov/.../recentresearchonlaborsupplyelasticities.pdf)
- **Thread:** A (elasticity survey anchor) + Thread B angle 1 (the parameters CBO uses for dynamic scoring)
- **Evidence class:** Review of measured estimates feeding an ex-ante modeling apparatus.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Updates CBO's 1996 review of how labor supply responds to after-tax wages.
Substitution elasticities exceed income elasticities in absolute value; the decision
to work (extensive margin) is more responsive to after-tax wages than the choice of
hours (intensive). Married women's elasticities have fallen substantially over three
decades but remain above those of men and single women.

## CBO central parameters — source document identified
The 0.19 / 0.24 / −0.05 central parameter set is **not** stated in this elasticity-review
working paper (43675), which surveys the literature without committing CBO to a single
central set. CBO's central operating parameters are stated in the companion October 2012
report **"How the Supply of Labor Responds to Changes in Fiscal Policy" (CBO publication
43674)** — distinct from pub 43680 ("How Does CBO Model the Response of Labor Supply...").
- **Substitution elasticity 0.24** — confirmed as CBO's Solow-model substitution
  elasticity (corroborated across CBO Oct 2012 materials and the Reichling–Whalen Frisch
  review, WP 2012-13 / pub 43676).
- **Income elasticity −0.05** — within the −0.05 to −0.11 range CBO cites from lottery /
  housing-voucher studies; −0.05 is the central value.
- **Aggregate/total (Marshallian) wage elasticity ≈ 0.19** — the earnings-weighted
  aggregate.

## Disaggregated elasticity ranges by group (Thread 3 — target-pool responsiveness)
The review reports the participation (extensive-margin) response is small for the 80-80's
core entry pool and large only for EITC-type single mothers:
- **Total/participation elasticity for men and single women: ~0 to 0.1.** Corroborated via
  multiple summaries of the review (e.g., the review gives ~0–0.1 for single men and single
  women; the substitution-elasticity range for men and single women is ~0.1–0.3).
- **Married women: wider, ~0 to 0.3** (participation), down sharply over three decades but
  still above men/single women.
- **Low-income / EITC-eligible (esp. single mothers): substantially higher.** The review
  notes low-income workers have higher elasticities; among EITC-eligibles, after-tax income
  boosted participation (esp. single mothers) with little hours effect. Specific anchors the
  review cites: **Chetty (2012) average participation elasticity 0.25** (single mothers/
  married women higher than the general population); **Card & Hyslop (2005) extensive-margin
  Frisch ~0.4 for single mothers.**

KEY MODELING IMPLICATION FOR 80-80: the EITC participation elasticities do NOT transfer to
childless prime-age men, the largest 80-80 entry pool, whose participation elasticity is
~0–0.1. The model's entry response for that pool should sit near the bottom of the band.

## Why it matters for 80-80
This is the official scoring-agency parameter set. A dynamic 80-80 cost model that
wants to mirror CBO convention would use a total wage elasticity near 0.19 as the
central case, with the substitution/income split to separate the work-incentive
effect from the income (leisure) effect of the wage fill.
