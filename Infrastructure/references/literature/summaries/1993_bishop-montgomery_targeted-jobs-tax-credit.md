# Bishop & Montgomery (1993) — Does the Targeted Jobs Tax Credit Create Jobs at Subsidized Firms?

- **Tier:** 1 (Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society)
- **Type:** paper
- **Source:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1468-232x.1993.tb01051.x
- **DOI:** 10.1111/j.1468-232x.1993.tb01051.x
- **Thread:** B angle 4 (U.S. employment/wage-subsidy program evaluation; New Jobs / Targeted Jobs Tax Credit lineage).
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — firm-level survey data on subsidized employers.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Uses firm-level data to ask whether the U.S. Targeted Jobs Tax Credit (TJTC) — the
categorical successor to the 1977–78 New Jobs Tax Credit — actually raised employment at
claiming firms or merely subsidized hiring that would have happened anyway (windfall). Finds
employment gains concentrated among certified workers at participating firms, but with
substantial windfall/displacement: much of the credit rewarded hires that would have
occurred regardless, and take-up was low. Net job creation per dollar is modest, consistent
with the broader finding that highly targeted categorical credits are inefficient job
creators.

## Why it matters for 80-80
This is the validated U.S. empirical anchor for the New Jobs / Targeted Jobs Tax Credit
evidence requested for Thread B angle 4. It documents the windfall and low-take-up problems
of *categorical, employer-side* subsidies — precisely the failure modes the 80-80's broad,
worker-side, per-hour design is meant to avoid. It pairs with Neumark & Grijalva (state
hiring credits) and Katz (1998, the survey) to make the design-dependence point: the
behavioral and fiscal payoff of a subsidy depends on who receives it and how it is
conditioned, not the headline elasticity alone.
