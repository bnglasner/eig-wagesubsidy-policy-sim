# Lee, Michelmore, Pilkauskas & Wimer (2024) — Effects of the Expansion of the Earned Income Tax Credit for Childless Young Adults on Material Wellbeing

- **Tier:** 1 (NBER Working Paper 32571, June 2024)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.2139/ssrn.4867435 (SSRN working-paper DOI; the NBER working-paper page itself
  does not issue a separate DOI)
- **Source:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w32571 ; SSRN: https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4867435
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — household-composition/childless-worker channel,
  2021 ARPA expansion)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — difference-in-differences using the Census
  Bureau's Household Pulse Survey around the 2021 American Rescue Plan Act (ARPA) childless-
  EITC expansion.

## Finding (paraphrased)
In 2021, ARPA temporarily expanded the federal childless-worker EITC — the same schedule
Nichols & Rothstein (2015, cataloged) describe as roughly 15 percent of the one-child
maximum before this change — by roughly tripling the maximum credit (contemporaneous
reporting cited elsewhere in this catalog's search puts this at approximately $538 to
$1,502) and lowering the minimum eligibility age from 25 to 19 (while also raising/removing
the upper age limit for older childless workers). Using a difference-in-differences design
comparing likely-eligible low-income childless young adults to less-affected comparison
groups, the authors find the expansion led to a **significant decrease in housing hardship**
among low-income childless young adults, and **suggestive (weaker) evidence** of reduced
food insufficiency and difficulty paying expenses. **This paper measures material hardship
outcomes, not employment or labor-supply outcomes** — it does not report an employment or
hours-worked coefficient for the ARPA childless-EITC expansion.

## Why this matters for the household-composition-channel question
This is the most directly relevant *academic* ex-post evaluation of the 2021 ARPA childless-
EITC expansion located this pass, and it confirms the policy change occurred as described
(tripled maximum, age 19 floor) and had real, measurable welfare effects on the newly
reached population. However, **it does not answer the specific question this literature
request is trying to resolve** — whether making the childless credit more generous (without
changing its still-categorical, still-lump-sum, still-one-year design) raised
employment/participation for childless adults, including men. Because the ARPA expansion
was temporary (one tax year), a companion caveat commonly raised in secondary discussion of
this literature (e.g., Congressional Research Service, CBPP commentary) is that any
employment response would likely be muted relative to a permanent expansion, since a one-
year windfall does not change the long-run return to labor-force attachment the way a
permanent schedule change would. `[unverified: no academic paper measuring the ARPA
childless-EITC expansion's employment/labor-supply effect specifically (as opposed to
material-hardship or income effects) was located and verified this pass — this is a real
gap in the literature as scouted, not a null finding to be cited as evidence either way.]`

## Caveats
- Outcome variable is material hardship (housing, food, expenses), not employment or hours
  — do not cite this paper for an employment-elasticity claim.
- The Household Pulse Survey is a rapid-response, non-longitudinal-panel survey instrument
  with known limitations (self-reported hardship measures, survey-mode effects); the
  authors' difference-in-differences design partially addresses this but the identification
  is not as clean as an RD or RCT.
- The ARPA expansion was temporary (tax year 2021 only), which likely limits how much any
  finding here (or absence of an employment finding) generalizes to a permanent policy
  change like the 80-80.
