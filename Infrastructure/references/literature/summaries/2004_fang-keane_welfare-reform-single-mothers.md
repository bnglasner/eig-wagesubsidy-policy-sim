## Findings (pinned from the BPEA PDF, main-findings section)
- Single mothers' **work participation rate rose 11.3 percentage points from 1993 to 2002**.
  Decomposition, in order of importance: **EITC 33 percent; macroeconomy 25 percent; work
  requirements 17 percent; time limits 10 percent**.
- Single mothers' **welfare participation rate fell 23 percentage points from 1993 to 2002**.
  Decomposition: **work requirements 57 percent; EITC 26 percent; time limits 11 percent;
  macroeconomy 7 percent**.
- Post-1996 the fraction of single mothers who work rose only from **74 percent (1996) to 79
  percent (2002)** — most of the employment rise came in the 1993-96 EITC-expansion + boom
  window, not after PRWORA.
- "Nearly one-quarter of welfare leavers actually did not start work" — leaving welfare and
  entering work are distinct margins; work requirements moved the former more than the latter.
- Time limits alone boosted low-educated mothers' employment by up to ~7pp 1995-99 (from a
  ~60 percent base, per the paper's discussion of prior estimates it builds on).

## Why it matters for 80-80 (Q2: pinning the macro-scale precedent)
This is the cleanest single decomposition of the 1990s episode. The bottom line for
calibrating a wage subsidy's entry margin: a **combination** of (i) a permanent several-
thousand-dollar-per-year increase in the net return to work (EITC), (ii) coercive sticks
(work requirements, time limits), and (iii) the strongest labor market in a generation
moved the single-mother employment rate by **~11pp over nine years** — and the wage-subsidy
component alone accounts for **~3.7pp** of that (33% of 11.3pp). Single mothers are the
*most* wage-responsive demographic in the entire literature (CBO/McClelland-Mok, cataloged);
every other group in the 80-80's 59M pool has measured responsiveness at or below this.
Any model whose induced-entry projection implies a per-dollar response far above this
benchmark for less responsive groups is aggressive, not conservative.
