# Blundell, Duncan, McCrae & Meghir (2000) — The Labour Market Impact of the Working Families' Tax Credit

- **Tier:** 1 (Fiscal Studies; IFS/UCL authors)
- **Type:** paper
- **Source:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1475-5890.2000.tb00581.x
- **DOI:** 10.1111/j.1475-5890.2000.tb00581.x
- **Thread:** B angle 4 (direct in-work-benefit program evaluation).
- **Evidence class:** Modeled/ex-ante — structural labor-supply microsimulation of a real reform.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Simulates the labor-supply effects of the UK Working Families' Tax Credit (WFTC), the
1999 reform that replaced Family Credit with a more generous in-work benefit. Using a
structural discrete-choice labor-supply model estimated on UK data, the authors project a
small net increase in overall participation: WFTC raises employment among single mothers
(extensive margin) but the larger awards and joint income test reduce labor supply of some
partnered women in two-earner couples (the second-earner disincentive). Net participation
effect is modestly positive but heterogeneous by family structure.

## Why it matters for 80-80
The WFTC is a leading non-U.S. in-work-benefit analog to the EITC/80-80 family, and this
paper supplies the canonical structural-simulation template: it shows that the headline
employment effect of an in-work credit is the *net* of opposing extensive-margin gains
(primary earners / single parents enter) and intensive- or second-earner *losses* (joint
income tests discourage spousal work). For the 80-80, whose eligibility runs through the
broad workforce including secondary earners, this is the key heterogeneity the cost model
must carry — a single average elasticity hides offsetting responses.

## Caveats
- Ex-ante structural simulation, not ex-post measurement; magnitudes hinge on the
  estimated discrete-choice elasticities.
- UK tax-credit and family-structure context; the 80-80 is an individual wage-fill, not a
  family-income-tested credit, so the second-earner channel may operate differently.
