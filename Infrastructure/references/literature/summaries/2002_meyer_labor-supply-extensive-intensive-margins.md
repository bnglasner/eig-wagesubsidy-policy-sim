# Meyer (2002) — Labor Supply at the Extensive and Intensive Margins: The EITC, Welfare, and Hours Worked

- **Tier:** 1 (American Economic Review, Papers and Proceedings)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1257/000282802320191642
- **Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/000282802320191642 ; Northwestern IPR working-paper version WP-02-04
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — re-verifying the "Meyer 2002" citation embedded in `00_config.py` and the two Agglomerations articles)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — reduced-form analysis of 1980s-90s tax/welfare reforms.

## Finding (paraphrased)
This is the specific Meyer (2002) paper the project's own articles and `00_config.py`
comments invoke (distinct from the longer Meyer & Rosenbaum 2001 QJE paper, already
cataloged). Using the sequence of EITC expansions, welfare reform, and other tax/transfer
changes in the 1980s-90s, Meyer decomposes the labor-supply response into an **extensive
margin (whether to work)** and an **intensive margin (hours conditional on working)**.
Central finding: for single mothers, essentially **all** of the measured labor-supply
response to the EITC and welfare reform operated through the **extensive margin** —
entry into employment — with **little to no intensive-margin (hours) response**. This is
the paper underlying the project's repeated framing that "the EITC's demonstrated effect
is a participation effect, not an hours effect."

## What this paper does NOT say (important for the decomposition question)
Meyer (2002) is a paper about **single mothers specifically** — it documents the
extensive/intensive split for the population EITC was designed around. It is not a
demographic-heterogeneity paper contrasting single mothers against childless men within
the same paper; the near-zero response for childless adults/men is documented elsewhere
(McClelland & Mok 2012, already cataloged, and the broader Hotz-Scholz survey, already
cataloged) as a feature of the EITC's **categorical eligibility** (childless workers were
excluded or only recently and minimally covered by the credit) rather than as a finding
of Meyer (2002) itself. Citing Meyer (2002) alone for "the EITC is near-zero for men" would
overstate what this specific paper measures; it is best cited for the **extensive-vs-
intensive decomposition for single mothers**, with McClelland & Mok / Hotz & Scholz
carrying the demographic-heterogeneity claim.

## Why it matters for 80-80 (and for the decomposition question)
Confirms the extensive-margin-dominant pattern the project's articles build on, but also
sharpens what is and is not attributable to this specific citation. Because Meyer's
sample is single mothers under the EITC/welfare-reform regime of that era — a population
facing a **specific package of categorical eligibility, phase-out design, and
overlapping welfare reform** — the paper cannot by itself separate "extensive margin
responds to income while intensive margin does not" (a general labor-supply-elasticity
regularity, see Chetty et al. and Blundell-MaCurdy, already cataloged) from "the EITC's
specific institutional design produced this pattern for this population" (an EITC-design
claim). Both readings are consistent with the paper; the paper alone cannot adjudicate
between them.
