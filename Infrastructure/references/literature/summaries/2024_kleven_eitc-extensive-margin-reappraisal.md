# Kleven (2024) — The EITC and the Extensive Margin: A Reappraisal

- **Tier:** 1 (Journal of Public Economics)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2024.105135
- **Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272724000719 ; NBER w26405 ; henrikkleven.com PDF
- **Thread:** A (the key CONFLICT in the EITC extensive-margin literature)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post reappraisal; ~500 event studies across all state and federal EITC reforms.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Reappraises the EITC extensive-margin consensus. Across roughly 500 event studies of
every state and federal EITC reform since inception, treatment-impact estimates are
**symmetrically distributed around zero**; specifications producing large elasticities
are outliers. The lone exception is the **federal 1993 expansion**, whose large
single-mother employment gains align more with **confounding welfare reform and the
1990s macroeconomy** than with the EITC itself. Conclusion: DiD/event-study evidence
is fragile to specification and does not robustly support large employment effects.

## Conflict note (central to Thread A)
Directly contradicts Eissa & Liebman (1996), Meyer & Rosenbaum (2001), and Hoynes &
Patel (2018). This is the single most important disagreement in the file: the headline
extensive-margin elasticity the cost model would lean on is itself contested.

## Why it matters for 80-80
If the EITC extensive-margin effect is much smaller/less robust than the consensus,
a dynamic 80-80 model that books large induced-employment tax recapture would overstate
self-financing. Argues for a wide sensitivity band on the entry elasticity, with a
near-zero lower bound, not just the Chetty/CBO central case.
