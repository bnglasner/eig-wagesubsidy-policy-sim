# Azmat (2019) — Incidence, Salience and Spillovers: The Direct and Indirect Effects of Tax Credits on Wages

- **Tier:** 1 (Quantitative Economics)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.3982/QE319
- **Source:** https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.3982/QE319 ; Econometric Society publications page
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — the key PAYMENT-SALIENCE / delivery-channel paper)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — quasi-experimental, exploits the UK Working
  Families' Tax Credit (WFTC, 1999) payment-channel design.

## Finding (paraphrased)
The UK's WFTC (predecessor to the current Working Tax Credit) was, unlike the EITC,
initially **paid through the employer** (added to the paycheck by the firm, which then
reclaimed the cost from the tax authority) rather than paid directly to the worker by the
government. This makes the credit **salient to the employer**, not just the worker. Azmat
separately identifies (i) the direct effect of a larger credit amount and (ii) the effect
of this **employer-salience** channel specifically, and finds that employers **cut the wage
of claimant workers by about 30 percent of the tax-credit value (roughly 7 percent of the
wage)** relative to observably similar non-claimant coworkers, with a **negative spillover
onto the wages of nearby non-claimant workers** as well.

## Why this matters for the decomposition question (directly on point for "salience")
This is the sharpest available evidence that **how** an in-work transfer is delivered —
not just its schedule or eligibility rules — changes its economic effect, because it
changes **who is made aware of it and can act on that awareness**. It cuts in a specific,
important direction for the 80-80: making a subsidy more "visible" is not unambiguously
good for the worker. Visibility to the **employer** (as opposed to visibility to the
**worker**, which the project's articles argue the 80-80 improves via per-paycheck framing)
can let the firm capture part of the transfer via wage suppression. This is a genuinely
different salience channel than "does the worker understand the schedule" (Chetty & Saez
2013; Bhargava & Manoli 2015, both in this catalog) — it is about **employer** salience and
**incidence**, and it is the clearest documented case of a design choice (payment channel)
producing a measured effect distinct from the credit's schedule generosity. It should be
read together with Rothstein (2010, already cataloged) on EITC wage incidence: both papers
show that a wage-linked transfer's net worker benefit depends on labor-market incidence
mechanisms that operate independently of the extensive-margin participation elasticity.

## Caveats
- This paper's finding is primarily about **wage incidence for existing/claimant workers**,
  not directly about the **extensive-margin entry decision** the 80-80 spec is trying to
  model — it is evidence on the wage-capture (02c/02d incidence) side of the 80-80's
  architecture at least as much as the entry side. Cite it for both the salience-mechanism
  point and as a caution for the incidence module, not solely as extensive-margin evidence.
- UK WFTC (1999-2003) institutional design (paid via PAYE payroll) has no direct US
  analog; the 80-80, as a worker-side transfer not routed through the employer's payroll
  system in the same reclaim structure, may or may not replicate this exact salience
  channel — flag as an open question for the incidence module rather than assume either
  direction.

  value" wage-cut estimate not pinned to a number this pass — abstract/secondary summary
