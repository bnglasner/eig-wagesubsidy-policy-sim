- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — noncustodial-father/male-nonemployment population)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — randomized controlled trial (RCT), seven sites,

## Finding (paraphrased)
Parents' Fair Share (PFS) is the canonical 1990s demonstration targeted at low-income
**noncustodial fathers** — men who are structurally excluded from the standard EITC because
their children do not live with them, the population most directly implicated in the
household-composition/custody channel. PFS was **not a wage or earnings subsidy**: it
combined employment and training services (largely job clubs), enhanced/modified child-
support enforcement, peer support groups, and voluntary mediation between parents. More than
5,500 fathers were randomly assigned to PFS or a control group across seven cities
(Springfield MA, Trenton NJ, Los Angeles CA, Dayton OH, Grand Rapids MI, Jacksonville FL,
Memphis TN). Results were **heterogeneous by disadvantage**: PFS "increased employment and
earnings for the least-employable men but not for the men who were more able to find work
on their own" — i.e., a services-based intervention moved the least job-ready subgroup but
had no detectable effect on more job-ready participants. PFS also significantly increased
the likelihood and amount of child support paid.

## Why this matters for the household-composition-channel question
PFS establishes that the population this project cares about (low-income, often
noncustodial, prime-age men outside stable employment) is **not uniformly unresponsive** to
intervention — a real, RCT-verified employment response exists for the most disadvantaged
subgroup. But it is important not to over-map this onto the 80-80/EITC comparison:
1. **PFS is a services-and-enforcement intervention, not an earnings subsidy.** It tests
   whether job-search assistance, training, and stronger child-support enforcement can move
   employment for excluded men — a different treatment than "give this population a
   worker-side wage top-up regardless of custody status." It is suggestive that this
   population is not intrinsically unmovable, which supports treating the EITC's near-zero
   estimate as *partly* an artifact of the EITC's specific instrument rather than a fixed
   fact about male labor supply — but it cannot be read as direct evidence that a *wage
   subsidy specifically* (as opposed to services/enforcement) would produce the same
   employment gain.
2. **The response was concentrated in the least-employable subgroup**, echoing the same
   heterogeneity-by-disadvantage pattern found in the Paycheck Plus NYC result
   ("disadvantaged men," including noncustodial parents and formerly incarcerated
   individuals, saw the largest gains). Two independent demonstrations, using different
   instruments (services vs. an earnings credit), both find that within "men," the
   responsive subgroup is the most disadvantaged/noncustodial slice, not men broadly. This
   is a consistent secondary finding worth carrying into the M2 band discussion: if the
   model's "men" cell could be split by disadvantage/custodial status, the evidence would
   support a materially higher elasticity for the disadvantaged/noncustodial sub-cell and a
   lower one for the rest — blending them into one "men" elasticity likely understates the
   heterogeneity the literature actually shows.

## Caveats
- Not a wage subsidy — the mechanism tested (job search/training/enforcement/mediation) is
  different in kind from both the EITC and the 80-80. Citing PFS as evidence about
  *earnings-subsidy* responsiveness specifically would overreach; it is evidence about
  *this population's* responsiveness to *some* intervention, which is a necessary but not
  sufficient condition for optimism about a wage-subsidy-specific channel.
- 1990s labor market and welfare-reform-era institutional context; child-support
  enforcement intensity and TANF-era incentives differ from the current low-wage labor
  market the 80-80 targets.
