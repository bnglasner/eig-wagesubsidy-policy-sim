# Busso, Gregory & Kline (2013) — Assessing the Incidence and Efficiency of a Prominent Place-Based Policy

- **Tier:** 1 (American Economic Review)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1257/aer.103.2.897
- **Source:** https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.103.2.897 ; NBER w16096
- **Thread:** B angle 3 (incidence & wage pass-through) — the CLOSEST US wage-credit analog for benchmarking how much of a wage subsidy reaches workers vs. is captured elsewhere. PRIORITY source.
- **Evidence class:** Measured (ex-post) reduced-form incidence study; quasi-experimental, using rejected and future applicants as controls.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Evaluates Round I of the federal urban **Empowerment Zone (EZ)** program — a wage/employment
tax credit for hiring zone residents — using confidential Census and business microdata,
with rejected and later-round applicant zones as controls. Findings:
- EZ designation **substantially increased employment** in zone neighborhoods.
- It **raised wages for local (zone-resident) workers**, with the wage gains worth
  **about $320 million per year**.
- **Population and housing rents stayed roughly stable**, implying the targeted
  neighborhoods had slack in the local housing market. Because rents did not rise, the
  subsidy was **not** capitalized into land values / captured by absentee landlords —
  it largely reached the intended local workers.
- **Efficiency costs were relatively modest**: the transfer to local workers was achieved
  without large deadweight loss or major migration distortions.

## Why it matters for 80-80 (incidence benchmark)
The single best real-world US analog for the incidence question. The EZ is a wage-linked
subsidy, and Busso et al. trace where the money landed: **wages of local workers rose and
rents did not**, so pass-through to the intended beneficiaries was high and landowner
capture was low *in this setting*. This is the empirical counterweight to the Rothstein
(2010) employer-capture concern: when local housing/labor markets have slack, a
wage-linked subsidy can largely accrue to workers rather than being competed away.
For the 80-80, this argues that the degree of employer/landowner capture is
**market-condition-dependent** (slack vs. tight, local vs. national), and motivates a
pass-through parameter that is bounded but uncertain, not a single fixed leak rate.

- **Caveat / scope:** EZs are *place-based* and *spatially bounded*; gains partly reflect
  geographic targeting and local slack. The 80-80 is a national, worker-side wage fill, so
  the EZ's high worker pass-through is an upper-ish benchmark for a favorable
  (slack-market) case, not a guaranteed national outcome.
