## Finding (paraphrased)
A randomized experiment at H&R Block gave half of 43,000 EITC-claiming tax filers simple,
personalized information about their position on the EITC schedule (phase-in, plateau, or
phase-out) and the marginal implications of earning more or less. **In the full sample,
information provision had no statistically significant effect on subsequent-year
earnings.** Exploring heterogeneity, the effect varied substantially **by tax preparer**:
some individual preparers' clients responded to the information (more bunching near the
refund-maximizing kink) while most did not, suggesting the preparer's own understanding
and communication — not the raw information itself — mediates any behavioral response.

## Why this matters for the decomposition question (an important complicating finding)
This is a **null result on the central premise of the salience hypothesis** taken alone:
simply making the EITC's schedule more legible to filers did not, on average, change
earnings behavior. This complicates a simple version of the project's articles' argument
that "the EITC's complexity/opacity is what mutes its effect, so a transparent formula
(the 80-80) will elicit more response." If pure informational salience were the dominant
mechanism, this experiment should have found an effect; it largely did not, except through
an indirect, preparer-mediated channel. The more optimistic salience story instead rests on
i) Bhargava & Manoli (2015, cataloged separately in this pass) — where salience mattered a
great deal for benefit **take-up** (claiming a credit already earned), a different margin
than **labor-supply/earnings** response — and ii) Azmat (2019, cataloged separately) — where
salience to a different party (the employer) changed wage incidence. Read together, these
three papers suggest **payment/formula salience to the worker may move take-up of an
already-earned benefit more reliably than it moves the underlying labor-supply/entry
decision** — a meaningful distinction for what the 80-80's per-paycheck design can and
cannot be expected to accomplish relative to the EITC.

## Caveats
- Sample is EITC filers already in the labor force and already earning (some
  self-employed), not non-employed individuals deciding whether to enter — this paper
  speaks to the **intensive-margin/bunching** response to salience, not directly to
  extensive-margin entry.
- Single-tax-season experiment (short horizon); does not capture longer-run learning or
  employer-side responses.
