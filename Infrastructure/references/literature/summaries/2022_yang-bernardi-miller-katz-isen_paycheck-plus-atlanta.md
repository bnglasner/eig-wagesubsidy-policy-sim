# Yang, Bernardi, Metz, Miller, Katz & Isen (2022) — An Earned Income Tax Credit That Works for Singles: Final Impact Findings from the Paycheck Plus Demonstration in Atlanta

(Author list corrected 2026-07-08: title page adds Rachael Metz, previously omitted here.)

- **Tier:** 2 (MDRC; rigorous RCT program evaluation, not a peer-reviewed journal article)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** none issued; registered identifier is **OPRE Report 2022-54** (verified from title page 2026-07-08)
- **Source:** https://www.mdrc.org/work/publications/earned-income-tax-credit-works-singles
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — household-composition/childless-worker channel)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — randomized controlled trial (RCT), a direct
  second-site replication of the New York City Paycheck Plus design (Miller et al. 2018,
  separately cataloged).

## Finding (paraphrased)
Replicates the Paycheck Plus design (an expanded, worker-side, earnings-linked credit for
childless/non-custodial workers, up to $2,000/year with an income cap of $30,000) in
Atlanta with roughly 2,000 eligible participants randomly assigned to treatment or control,
followed for three years. In sharp contrast to the New York City result: Atlanta found
**no employment effects in any year, for any subgroup** — no significant increase in
employment for women, for "disadvantaged" or noncustodial men, or overall. After-tax income
increased in year one from the bonus payment itself but showed no employment/earnings
response, and by years two and three even the after-tax income effect had faded.

## Why this matters for the household-composition-channel question
This is the direct empirical counterweight to the NYC result (Miller et al. 2018) and
must be read together with it, not separately. The two sites tested essentially the same
non-categorical, worker-side, earnings-linked credit — the intervention structurally
closest to the 80-80's own non-categorical design among all wage-subsidy-adjacent evidence
in this catalog — and got **opposite answers on whether removing categorical/generosity
constraints raises employment for childless and noncustodial men**: NYC found a real,
subgroup-concentrated positive effect (up to 5.8 points for disadvantaged men); Atlanta
found a precise null across the board. Plausible explanations include differences in local
labor-market tightness, local wage levels relative to the $30,000 income cap, differences in
outreach/marketing intensity, or genuine site-specific heterogeneity in the population's
labor-supply elasticity — the reports do not resolve which. **The honest reading of this
pair of studies together is that a well-identified, non-categorical wage/earnings credit for
childless and noncustodial men can, but does not reliably, produce a larger employment
response than the EITC's own near-zero pattern implies — the evidence is genuinely
two-sided, not a one-directional confirmation.** This is exactly the kind of finding that
argues for a wide uncertainty band (per the project's M2 resolution) rather than for
raising a central estimate.

## Caveats
- Same design limitations as the NYC report: lump-sum tax-time delivery (not per-paycheck),
  voluntary/marketed take-up, and a targeted "disadvantaged" definition that does not map
  cleanly onto a general "childless men" population.
- Smaller sample (~2,000) than NYC (~6,000), which reduces power to detect subgroup effects
  even if a smaller true effect existed; a null result here is less informative against a
  small-but-real effect than the NYC-scale sample would be.

3. **Sample size was understated:** the study recruited **just over 4,000** single adults

### The null employment result — pinned with SEs (Table ES.1; IRS records, N = 3,972)
"Any earnings" effects in percentage points, program minus control (SE; p-value):
- **Full sample:** Year 1 +0.1 (1.1; 0.923); Year 2 +1.0 (1.2; 0.407); Year 3 +1.1
  (1.2; 0.355); ever employed Years 1-3 −0.9 (1.0; 0.324). Year 1 = tax year 2016,
  Year 3 = tax year 2018.
- **More disadvantaged men** (same definition as NYC: noncustodial parents at random
  assignment or ever incarcerated; Table 7; N = 787/834): Year 1 −0.3 (1.8; 0.856);
  Year 2 +0.7 (2.0; 0.722); Year 3 +0.8 (2.0; 0.698) — a precise null exactly where NYC
  found +5.8pp\*\*.
- **Women** (Table 8; N = 795/759): Year 1 +0.5 (1.6; 0.747); Year 2 −0.9 (1.8; 0.632);
  Year 3 0.0 (1.9; 0.991) — null where NYC found +4.6pp\*\*\*.
- **After-bonus earnings (full sample):** Year 1 **+$775 (SE 266; p = 0.004)** — the
  mechanical income gain from the bonus itself; Year 2 +$505 (332; 0.128); Year 3 +$16
  (395; 0.967). Confirms the prior entry's "income rose in year one from the bonus, then
  faded" characterization.
- **The program was delivered and taken up:** tax filing rose 12.1/9.8/9.0pp
  (all p < 0.001) and VITA-site filing rose ~17-23pp across years — the null is not a
  null of implementation failure in the mechanical sense, though the report attributes
  part of the NYC-Atlanta difference to operational and engagement challenges.

### One nuance the prior entry smooths over
The report states the program "did not produce differential effects on employment or
earnings for any specific subgroup, **with the exception of a pattern of larger earnings
effects for less disadvantaged men**" in the early years (Table 7, other men: after-bonus
earnings Year 1 +$1,656, p = 0.017; Year 2 +$2,070, p = 0.015; average earnings Year 2
+$1,875, p = 0.052), which had faded and lost significance by Year 3. The blanket
"no employment effects in any year, for any subgroup" is confirmed **for employment**;
for *earnings*, the early positive pattern among less disadvantaged men should be
disclosed when this null is used as load-bearing.
