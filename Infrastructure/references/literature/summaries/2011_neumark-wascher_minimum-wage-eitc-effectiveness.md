# Neumark & Wascher (2011) — Does a Higher Minimum Wage Enhance the Effectiveness of the Earned Income Tax Credit?

- **Tier:** 1 (ILR Review, Cornell University ILR School; also circulated as NBER Working
  Paper 12915)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1177/001979391106400405
- **Source:** https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/001979391106400405 ;
  NBER WP 12915: https://www.nber.org/papers/w12915
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — household-composition/childless-worker channel)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — difference-in-differences using state EITC and
  state minimum-wage variation, Current Population Survey data.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Estimates the interaction between state EITC generosity and state minimum-wage levels on
employment and earnings, by demographic group. Confirms the standard result that EITC
generosity raises employment and earnings for single mothers, and that this effect is
*reinforced* by a higher minimum wage (which helps prevent employers from capturing the
credit through lower wages, per the incidence mechanism in Rothstein 2010 and Nichols &
Rothstein 2015, both cataloged). But for groups who are *not* EITC-eligible and who compete
in the same low-wage labor market as EITC recipients — specifically **less-skilled minority
men and women without children** — the combination of a higher EITC and a higher minimum
wage is associated with **adverse employment and earnings effects**. The authors interpret
this as consistent with a binding-minimum-wage labor market: EITC-induced labor supply from
eligible workers increases, but a binding minimum wage prevents the wage decline that would
otherwise clear the market, so employment adjusts instead — rationing jobs away from close
substitutes who are not eligible for the credit.

## Why this matters for the household-composition-channel question
This is direct, measured evidence on the specific population the user's hypothesis
concerns — childless men and women — but it identifies a **different** channel than credit-
schedule generosity: a **general-equilibrium spillover/crowd-out** effect operating through
the minimum wage, not through the childless credit's own smallness. It matters for the
80-80 comparison in two ways:
1. It reinforces that childless/ineligible workers can be *harmed*, not merely
   under-helped, by a categorical credit that raises the effective labor supply of eligible
   (parent) workers in the same market — another mechanism (on top of Nichols-Rothstein's
   incidence discussion) by which the EITC's categorical structure disadvantages childless
   men specifically, independent of the childless credit's own size.
2. Because the 80-80 does *not* categorically exclude childless workers, it would not
   generate this particular spillover-crowd-out dynamic *from itself* — but the same
   minimum-wage-interaction mechanism could still apply to the 80-80 in reverse (if the
   80-80 raises effective labor supply broadly and a binding minimum wage prevents wage
   adjustment, incumbents and new entrants could still see incidence effects). This is a
   caution about minimum-wage interaction generally, not a decomposition of the EITC's own
   design specifically.

## Caveats
- Reduced-form difference-in-differences across states and years; identifies an average
  interaction effect, not a structural decomposition of *why* childless workers are
  affected (crowd-out vs. direct exclusion).
- The adverse effect is specifically on the *interaction* of EITC generosity with a *higher*
  minimum wage — it does not by itself say what happens to childless workers under an EITC
  expansion (or an 80-80-style subsidy) absent a binding minimum-wage constraint.
