# Katz (1998) — Wage Subsidies for the Disadvantaged

- **Tier:** 1 (chapter in *Generating Jobs*, eds. Freeman & Gottschalk, Russell Sage Foundation; Harvard/NBER author)
- **Type:** paper
- **Source:** chapter pp. 21–53 in *Generating Jobs: How to Increase Demand for Less-Skilled Workers* (Russell Sage, 1998); working-paper version NBER w5679 (1996), https://www.nber.org/papers/w5679
- **DOI (working paper):** 10.3386/w5679
- **Thread:** B angle 4 (the canonical economics survey of wage subsidies).
- **Evidence class:** Survey of measured estimates + design framework.

## Finding (paraphrased)
Lays out the basic economics of wage subsidies, distinguishes design variants
(employer-side hiring credits vs. worker-side earnings/wage subsidies; categorical/targeted
vs. broad; marginal/incremental vs. stock-based), and reviews evidence on U.S. wage-subsidy
programs and demonstrations. Core conclusions: subsidies to employers to hire disadvantaged
workers modestly raise labor demand for those workers, but highly targeted stand-alone
subsidies suffer low take-up and can stigmatize recipients, blunting effectiveness; broad,
non-categorical designs and earnings supplements paid to workers avoid the stigma problem
but are costlier per job. The New Jobs Tax Credit (a temporary, marginal, non-categorical
employer subsidy) is cited as comparatively effective at the margin.

## Why it matters for 80-80
This is the organizing survey for Thread B angle 4: it provides the taxonomy that locates
the 80-80 (a broad, worker-side, per-hour wage fill — not targeted, not employer-side) and
the central design lesson that targeting/conditioning, take-up, and stigma — not just the
elasticity — drive whether a subsidy changes employment. It supplies the framework for why
the 80-80's broad, worker-paid design sidesteps the take-up/stigma failures that sank
narrowly targeted credits like the TJTC.
