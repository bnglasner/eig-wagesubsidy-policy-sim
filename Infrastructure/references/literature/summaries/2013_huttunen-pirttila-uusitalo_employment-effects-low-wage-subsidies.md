# Huttunen, Pirttilä & Uusitalo (2013) — The Employment Effects of Low-Wage Subsidies

- **Tier:** 1 (Journal of Public Economics)
- **Type:** paper
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2012.09.007
- **Source:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272712001065 ; IZA DP 4931
- **Thread:** 6 (EITC-design-decomposition — direct WAGE-SUBSIDY, non-EITC evidence on the extensive margin)
- **Evidence class:** Measured/ex-post — quasi-experimental (age/wage/hours eligibility
  discontinuities in a real Finnish payroll-tax subsidy).

## Finding (paraphrased)
Evaluates a Finnish payroll-tax subsidy targeted at **employers** of older
(54+), full-time, low-wage workers — a genuine wage-subsidy program, not an EITC-style
worker credit. Using the program's age, wage, and hours eligibility thresholds for
identification, the authors find the subsidy had **no detectable effect on the
employment rate or wages** of the targeted group, but did **slightly increase working
hours among workers who were already employed** (an intensive-margin effect on
incumbents, not an extensive-margin entry effect).

## Why this matters for the decomposition question (directly on point)
This is a *wage subsidy*, structurally closer to the 80-80 family than the EITC, and it
finds **essentially zero extensive-margin (entry) response** — the opposite of what a
naive "wage subsidies should beat the EITC on entry because they lack its frictions"
prior would predict. Two readings are both defensible and should be disclosed together:
1. **Design-specific reading:** this subsidy was paid to *employers*, not workers, so any
   demand-side incidence effect (higher effective marginal product) does not translate
   one-for-one into a worker-perceived wage gain — the salience/take-home-pay channel the
   80-80 relies on (per-paycheck, worker-visible) is absent here. The null result may be a
   feature of *this* subsidy's employer-side delivery, not evidence against wage subsidies
   generally.
2. **General-constraint reading:** targeted at *already-employed-adjacent*, older,
   full-time low-wage workers, a population that may face binding non-wage constraints
   (health, caregiving, local labor demand) that a wage-linked incentive cannot move
   regardless of delivery design — i.e., a null result consistent with a general
   labor-supply-constraint story, not an EITC-specific-design story.

This paper is therefore evidence **against** an unqualified claim that removing EITC-style
frictions (annual payment, formula complexity, categorical eligibility) is sufficient to
generate a larger extensive-margin response — at least for an employer-side subsidy design
and this population. It is a caution belonging alongside the more optimistic wage-subsidy
evidence in the catalog (Card-Hyslop 2005 SSP), not a substitute for it.

## Household-composition conditioning (2026-07-08 addendum, requested re-verification)
**Confirmed: NONE.** Eligibility for this Finnish payroll-tax subsidy runs on the
*employer's* payment of an *age-54-or-older, full-time, low-wage employee* — age, hours,
and wage-level thresholds only. There is no test on the worker's marital status, presence
of children, or household composition anywhere in the program design as described in the
paper's abstract and summary treatments. This means the null extensive-margin result
**cannot** be attributed to a categorical/children-based exclusion (the channel the 80-80
is structurally different from the EITC on) — the subsidy was already non-categorical by
family status. The two live candidate explanations for the null remain (i) the
employer-side delivery channel (below) and (ii) a general labor-supply constraint in this
specific (older, already-employed-adjacent) population, not a household-composition
channel.

## Worker vs. employer payment (2026-07-08 addendum, requested re-verification)
**Confirmed: EMPLOYER-side.** This is a payroll-tax subsidy paid to the *employer* of a
qualifying worker, not a credit or payment delivered to the worker. This is a
fundamentally different mechanism from a worker-facing, reservation-wage-affecting subsidy
(and from the 80-80, which is worker-visible/per-paycheck by the project's own design): a
hiring-cost subsidy changes labor *demand* incentives and only reaches the worker's
effective wage to the extent employers pass the subsidy through, whereas a worker-side
payment changes the worker's own take-home pay and reservation-wage calculation directly.
**Implication for citing this paper against the 80-80's "men" case:** because both the
delivery channel (employer- vs. worker-side) and the categorical-conditioning question are
confirmed to differ from the 80-80's design along the *delivery* axis only (not the
categorical-eligibility axis, which was already absent here), this paper is informative
about "does a non-worker-visible subsidy fail to move entry" — a genuine caution — but it
is **not** informative about "does removing EITC's household-composition conditioning
specifically raise male entry," because this subsidy never had that conditioning to
remove. Citing it for the former question is well-founded; citing it for the latter
(household-composition-specific) question would overreach.

## Caveats
- Employer-side payroll subsidy, not a worker-side wage fill — the delivery mechanism
  differs from the 80-80 in exactly the dimension (worker-visible, per-paycheck) the
  project's articles argue matters most.
- Population is older (54+) full-time low-wage workers, not the broad working-age pool
  (including entrants from nonemployment) the 80-80 targets.
- Not categorical by household composition (age/wage/hours-based only) — see addendum
  above. This paper speaks to the delivery-channel question, not the
  household-composition/childless-conditioning question.

## Primary-source verification (2026-07-08, second pass)

IZA DP 4931 (docs.iza.org/dp4931.pdf, May 2010, 34 pp.) downloaded and read in full.
Independent fresh verification, not relying on the earlier addenda above.

### Delivery and eligibility — independently re-confirmed
- **EMPLOYER-side: confirmed verbatim.** Section 3: "The wage subsidy is paid to the
  employer and can be seen as simply a reduction in the payroll tax rate for the firms
  that employ old low-wage workers." In force from January 1, 2006 (temporary, through
  December 2010).
- **Eligibility (Section 3): worker must be over 54 years old, employed at least 140
  hours/month (full-time), with monthly wage between 900 and 2,000 euros.** No household,
  marital, or children conditioning anywhere in the eligibility rules — the earlier
  "non-categorical by family status" addendum is confirmed.
- **Subsidy schedule:** 44 percent of the part of the monthly wage exceeding 900 euros,
  maximum 220 euros/month, phased out at 55 percent of wages above 1,600 euros; at most
  ~16 percent of the gross wage (~13 percent of pre-reform total labor cost; average
  payroll tax rate 20.9 percent in 2006). Take-up among eligible workers' employers was
  incomplete (64 percent in the service sector).

### Point estimates — now pinned (previously flagged unverified)
- **Employment rate (Labour Force Survey, DiD):** the raw post-reform difference for
  eligible age groups is +2.8pp, but it is driven by a general employment increase among
  older cohorts — "after adding cohort-specific trends to the equations the estimated
  effects decrease close to zero and are not significant in any specification" (Section 4,
  Table 1). **Null confirmed.**
- **Re-employment of unemployed 54+ (DDD, census panel, Table 2):** +1 percent, not
  statistically significant (education-based treatment definition); the previous-wage-based
  definition gives a larger but still insignificant estimate. **No extensive-margin entry
  effect confirmed.**
- **Hours (employer payroll records, DDD, Table 6 area):** working hours of older
  low-wage workers in the **industrial sector** rose by almost **4.5 percent** measured
  by eligibility (about **1.8 percent** when scaled by the actual subsidy amount); **no
  hours effect in the service sector**. Footnote 19: the subsidy raised the probability
  that a **part-time** worker became **full-time** by ~7 percent in the industrial
  sector, and had **no impact on the hours of workers who were already full-time**.
- **Hourly wages (stayers, Table 7):** roughly a 2 percent *decrease* in the industrial
  sector; little/no effect in services (monthly wages rose because hours rose).
- **Conclusions (Section 8, verbatim gist):** "the subsidy scheme was not effective in
  increasing the employment of eligible workers," with a possible hours increase in the
  industrial sector "by making some former part-time workers work full-time."

### Correction to prior characterization
The prior Finding section said the subsidy "slightly increase[d] working hours among
workers who were already employed (an intensive-margin effect on incumbents)." Correct as
far as it goes, with two sharpenings now pinned to the source: (1) the hours effect is
**industrial-sector only** and operates through **part-time incumbents converting to
full-time** — already-full-time workers' hours did not change; (2) the extensive-margin
null is confirmed at both margins tested (stock employment rate, and re-employment of the
unemployed). No prior number in this entry was wrong.
