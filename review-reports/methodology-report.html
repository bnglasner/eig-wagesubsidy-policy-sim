Executive Summary

Overall Methodological Risk: MEDIUM
This review covers the newly internalized CPS ORG wage-construction path: the three vendored EIG-Wage-Figure R stages (00a/01a/01b) plus the Python stages rewired to consume the EIG-native panel (01a_data_ingest.py, 01h_nonemployed_pool.py) and 00_config.py. The project is a survey-weighted descriptive policy microsimulation — there is no causal identification design (no DiD/RD/IV), so findings concentrate in Sample & Data, Standard Errors, Descriptive Analysis, and Dataset Variable Usage.

Two focus-area conclusions are affirmatively verified. (1) The vendored subset's legacy/*2 seam, the 2023m4–2024m12 rotation-group bridge, and the sex-separate Pareto topcode are genuinely inert for the 2025–2026 nominal window: the shipped diagnostics show skipped_post_pareto_max_year for every sex-year cell, only year=2025/year=2026 partitions exist (legacy rows = 0), and the bridge fires only for yr %in% c(2023, 2024). (2) The weighting contract is correct per the dataset registry: EARNWT (ORG earnings weight) drives the employed-earner wage/eligibility/cost statistics in 01a, and WTFINL (basic-monthly population weight) drives the non-employed pool in 01h — the correct weight-by-context split. MR-VU1 and MR-VU2 therefore pass.

Six MEDIUM concerns warrant author confirmation: a cross-stage TARGET_WAGE inconsistency (dynamic in 01a vs. a static 16.80 in 01h), the per-year RF hours imputation trained on partial calendar years inside the pooling window, the full-year annualization assumption behind annual_hours, the absence of any sampling-variance/replicate-weight quantification on the headline numbers, the Heckman exclusion restriction, and a nominal $0.50 floor that sits below the deflated EPI real floor. Five LOW items round out the report. No HIGH findings: the identification is descriptive, the inert-window claims hold, and the weight assignment is correct.

Causal Inference
MR-001
Heckman exclusion restriction (marital status, number of children) is questionable
code/01_data_preparation/01h_nonemployed_pool.py · Causal Inference
MEDIUM
Relevant Code
if not wage_eq:
    cols["married"]  = df["MARST"].isin([1, 2]).to_numpy().astype(float)  # exclusion restriction
    cols["nchild"]   = df["NCHILD"].to_numpy(float)                       # exclusion restriction
    cols["has_child"]= (df["NCHILD"].to_numpy(float) >= 1).astype(float)
Why This Is a Concern
The non-employed potential wage (MPL) is imputed with a two-step Heckman in which marital status and number of children enter the selection (participation) equation but are excluded from the wage equation. Valid identification requires these variables to affect participation but not the wage. The labor literature documents a marriage wage premium and a motherhood wage penalty, so both plausibly enter the wage equation directly. If the exclusion restriction fails, the selection correction is identified only off the nonlinearity of the inverse-Mills ratio (functional form), which is fragile and can yield unstable selection-corrected predictions. Because the imputed MPL then drives reservation wages and the elasticity-calibrated viable share, the induced-employment and net-cost estimates inherit this assumption. See the Heckman (1979) selection framework and standard critiques of weak/absent exclusion restrictions.
What to Check
  • Justify why marital status and children shift participation but not the offered wage in this population, or cite a source.
  • Report the IMR coefficient and its significance (already printed) and test sensitivity to dropping the IMR term (naive OLS) and to the cell-median fallback.
  • Assess whether the MPL distribution (p25/p50/p75) is stable across reasonable exclusion sets before relying on the non-employed cost contribution.

MR-002
No sampling variance on headline estimates; replicate weights unused; repeated persons treated as independent
code/01_data_preparation/01a_data_ingest.py, 01h_nonemployed_pool.py · Standard Errors
MEDIUM
Relevant Code
n_months = eligible.groupby(["year", "month"]).ngroups
eligible["weight"] = eligible["earnwt"] / n_months
...
total_workers_mn = df["weight"].sum() / 1e6
gross_cost_bn    = (df["subsidy_annual"] * df["weight"]).sum() / 1e9
Why This Is a Concern
The headline eligibility count (~25–30M) and gross cost (~$40–60B) are reported as point estimates with no confidence interval. CPS ORG is a complex probability sample; IPUMS/Census publish replicate weights precisely so that sampling variance can be quantified, and neither stage declares a survey design or bootstraps standard errors (registry MR-VU3 "replicate weights" pitfall). Separately, pooling 12 monthly person-month files induces repeated observations of the same individual across the 4-8-4 rotation (same CPSIDP can enter as MISH 4 and MISH 8, and the non-employed pool draws all rotations), so treating person-months as independent understates variance. Dividing by n_months correctly rescales the weight to a monthly-average population, but does nothing for the induced within-person correlation. For a public-facing cost figure, an unreported margin of error is a meaningful omission (Lumley 2010, Complex Surveys).
What to Check
  • Add a sampling-variance estimate for eligibility and cost via CPS replicate weights (or a survey-design bootstrap over months/persons).
  • Confirm whether any individual (CPSIDP) appears more than once in the pooled eligible sample within the 12-month window and quantify the duplication.
  • Report at least an approximate CI or state explicitly that sampling uncertainty is not quantified and why (interactive/descriptive scope).

MR-003
Cross-stage TARGET_WAGE inconsistency: dynamic in 01a, static $16.80 in 01h
code/01_data_preparation/01a_data_ingest.py, 01h_nonemployed_pool.py, code/00_setup/00_config.py · Sample & Data
MEDIUM
Relevant Code
# 01a_data_ingest.py — target computed from the NEW window's data
median_wage = _weighted_median(ph["hourly_wage_epi"].values, ph["earnwt"].values)
TARGET_WAGE = round(median_wage * TARGET_PCT, 2)

# 01h_nonemployed_pool.py — target taken from static config
TARGET_WAGE = float(cfg.get("ws_target_wage", 16.80))   # 00_config.py: 0.80 * 21.00
Why This Is a Concern
The employed-eligibility stage (01a) recomputes TARGET_WAGE dynamically as 80% of the weighted median paid-hourly wage in the new pooled window, while the non-employed pool (01h) reads the hard-coded ws_target_wage = 16.80 from config (derived from an assumed $21.00 median). If the new 2025–2026 window's median differs from $21.00, the two halves of the same policy simulation evaluate workers against different target thresholds — the employed against the data-driven target and the non-employed against a stale one. That is internally incoherent for a single policy and would bias the relative sizes of the employed vs. induced-entrant cost components. The whole point of internalizing a fresh window is that the median can move; the static config value should be regenerated or the two stages should share one computed target.
What to Check
  • Print the dynamic TARGET_WAGE from 01a for the current window and compare it to the config 16.80; confirm they coincide.
  • Have 01h (and 00_config.py) consume the single target computed in 01a rather than a hard-coded constant, or regenerate the config value each run.
  • If they diverge, treat this as HIGH: re-run the non-employed pool and cost aggregation on the correct target.
MR-004
Per-year RF hours imputation trained on partial calendar years inside the 12-month pool
code/00_ingest/01b_build-org-panel.R · Sample & Data
MEDIUM
Relevant Code
for (yr in years_available_int) {
  ...
  rf_fit <- ranger::ranger(target_num ~ ., data = rf_train_df,
                           case.weights = rf_train_weights_num, seed = 42L,
                           num.threads = 1L)   # fit per calendar year
Why This Is a Concern
The "hours vary"/"don't know" RF hours imputation is fit per calendar year. Under the recent 12-month window the year=2025 partition holds only the window's 2025 months and year=2026 only ~5 months, so each RF trains on a partial-year, seasonally non-representative slice rather than the full calendar year a companion full-history run would use. The author's own comparison shows ~2.4% of rows (the imputed-hours records: 1,620 in 2025, 1,070 in 2026 per hours_rf_diagnostics.csv) receive different imputed hours (mean |Δ| ≈ 1.3 weekly hours) purely from this windowing choice, and OOB R² is modest (0.27–0.30). Because imputed hours feed both the salaried hourly wage (weekly / hours) and annual_hours = hours × weeks_multiplier, the divergence propagates into baseline_income and the gross-cost estimate. The aggregate effect is likely small, but the calendar-year split is arbitrary relative to the pooling window and is not documented as a sensitivity check in the shipped pipeline.
What to Check
  • Quantify the aggregate cost/eligibility delta between the per-year RF and a single RF pooled over the full 12-month window; document it.
  • Consider fitting one RF over the pooled window (or over full calendar years) so imputed hours do not depend on where the window boundary falls.
  • Given OOB R² ≈ 0.3, report how sensitive annual-hours totals are to the imputed 2.4% of rows.
MR-005
Annualization via a fixed WKSTAT weeks-multiplier assumes full-year attachment
code/01_data_preparation/01a_data_ingest.py · Sample & Data
MEDIUM
Relevant Code
eligible["weeks_multiplier"] = wkstat_vals.map(WKSTAT_WEEKS).fillna(DEFAULT_WEEKS).astype(int)
eligible["annual_hours"]     = eligible["hours_epi"] * eligible["weeks_multiplier"]
eligible["baseline_income"]  = eligible["employer_wage"] * eligible["annual_hours"]
Why This Is a Concern
Annual hours (and therefore baseline income and the annual subsidy/cost) are built from a single point-in-time CPS ORG observation: usual weekly hours × a constant weeks-per-year mapped from the respondent's current WKSTAT status (52/48/40). This assumes the worker holds that labor-force status for the full year and ignores unemployment spells, mid-year job changes, and partial-year work. For a cost estimate that multiplies through annual hours, part-year workers surveyed while employed will have overstated annual hours and income. This is the established EIG method and is unchanged by the internalization, but it is a core measurement assumption behind the cost figure and should be disclosed and, ideally, bounded.
What to Check
  • State the full-year-attachment assumption in the public methodology note and, if possible, benchmark annual hours against an ASEC weeks-worked distribution.
  • Report how the gross cost changes under a weeks-worked haircut for economically part-time / not-at-work categories (WKSTAT 14/21/22/42).
  • Confirm the DEFAULT_WEEKS = 52 fallback is not silently absorbing a material share of rows.

MR-006
Nominal $0.50 floor sits below the deflated EPI $0.50 (1989 PCE) real floor
code/01_data_preparation/01a_data_ingest.py · Dataset Variable Usage
MEDIUM
Relevant Code
NOMINAL_HOURLY_FLOOR = 0.50   # "nominal approximation of the EPI $0.50 (1989 PCE) lower bound"
nominal_hourly = nominal_hourly.where(nominal_hourly >= NOMINAL_HOURLY_FLOOR)
...
org["employer_wage"] = org["hourly_wage_epi"].clip(lower=FEDERAL_MIN_WAGE)  # 7.25 floor
Why This Is a Concern
Omitting the EPI $200 upper bound is fully defensible for a <$16.80 target population (those workers are excluded by employer_wage < TARGET regardless), and this is confirmed. The lower bound is the issue: EPI's $0.50 floor is expressed in 1989 PCE dollars, which is roughly $1.2–$1.3 in 2025 nominal terms. Applying a literal $0.50 nominal floor is therefore more permissive than the EPI-intended real floor, so measured wages in roughly [$0.50, $1.25] (often salaried weekly / imputed-hours artifacts) survive the clean, are then clipped up to the $7.25 federal minimum, and are counted as eligible at $7.25 with a large subsidy. This can modestly inflate the eligible count and cost with what are effectively data-error records. The direction of the omission (too low, not too high) is what makes it worth confirming.
What to Check
  • Deflate the EPI $0.50 (1989 PCE) floor to a sample-period nominal value (~$1.2–$1.3) and re-apply, or document why the literal $0.50 is intended.
  • Count how many rows fall in [$0.50, $7.25] before the federal-minimum clip and confirm they are not disproportionately salaried low-hours artifacts.
  • Report the sensitivity of the eligible count/cost to the floor choice.

MR-007
Heckman second-stage SEs uncorrected for the generated IMR regressor; Duan smearing assumes homoskedastic retransformation
code/01_data_preparation/01h_nonemployed_pool.py · Regression Specification
LOW
Relevant Code
ols   = sm.OLS(y, Xw.to_numpy()).fit()          # Xw includes imr (a generated regressor)
smear = float(np.mean(np.exp(ols.resid)))       # Duan smearing (single global factor)
Why This Is a Concern
The wage OLS includes the inverse-Mills ratio, a regressor generated from a first-stage probit; conventional OLS standard errors on that stage are understated (they ignore first-stage estimation error — the standard two-step Heckman SE correction or a bootstrap is required). A single global Duan (1983) smearing factor also assumes the log-wage residual variance is constant across the covariate space; if it is not, the level-scale retransformation is biased. Impact here is limited because only point predictions are used to impute MPL (no inference is reported off this stage), so this is LOW — but if any SE or significance claim is ever drawn from these coefficients it should be corrected.
What to Check
  • If coefficients/SEs are ever reported, use two-step Heckman SEs or bootstrap the full two-step.
  • Consider cell-wise smearing if residual variance differs materially by education/sex/age.

MR-008
Nominal wages pooled across a 12-month inflationary window without within-window deflation
code/01_data_preparation/01a_data_ingest.py · Sample & Data
LOW
Relevant Code
median_wage = _weighted_median(ph["hourly_wage_epi"].values, ph["earnwt"].values)
TARGET_WAGE = round(median_wage * TARGET_PCT, 2)   # single median over ~12 pooled nominal months
Why This Is a Concern
The nominal-subsidy choice (no PCE deflation) is disclosed and defensible for a single-year policy target. However, pooling ~12–13 monthly samples spanning 2025m5–2026m5 into one weighted median mixes nominal wages measured across a period with ~2–3% inflation, so late-window wages are mechanically a couple of percent higher than early-window wages. The effect on a median-based target is small (medians are robust and the drift is <3%), but the within-window nominal drift is an undocumented modeling choice under the MR-VU3 "CPI variant / deflator" family — here the choice is explicitly "no deflation," which is fine, but the within-window mixing is not addressed.
What to Check
  • Note in the methodology that wages are pooled nominally across the window and quantify the max within-window price drift.
  • Optionally normalize all months to the window's midpoint nominal level before computing the median.

Why This Is a Concern
The non-employed pool is built from the last two year partitions via a hard-coded [-2:] slice. For the current 2025–2026 window this captures the entire 12-month sample and is correct. But it is a silent, un-parameterized restriction: if the recent-window scope ever spans three calendar years (e.g., a Nov–Oct window crossing a year boundary such that three year= partitions exist), 01h would silently drop the earliest partition while 01a (which globs all partitions) would not, desynchronizing the employed and non-employed samples. What to Check
  • Read all year partitions (like 01a) or derive the slice from the configured window length, rather than a fixed [-2:].
  • Add an assertion that the months loaded by 01h match those loaded by 01a.
